A storm door usually costs about $350 to $1,000 in 2026, installed, for a standard full-view or retractable-screen door from brands like Andersen, Larson or Pella. Labor alone typically runs $100 to $500. Heavy security doors, custom sizes and doors on out-of-square or rotted frames cost more. A storm door is one of the simpler door jobs, but it only works well when the opening it mounts to is square and solid.

## The numbers

These ranges come from 2026 national cost guides and installer pricing, which vary a lot.

| Door type | Typical installed cost |
|---|---|
| Basic aluminum storm door, stock size | $300 to $550 |
| Full-view door with interchangeable glass and screen | $400 to $800 |
| Retractable-screen door (screen rolls into the frame) | $500 to $1,000 |
| Heavy-duty or security storm door | $600 to $1,500+ |
| Custom size | add $200 to $600+ |
| Labor only, standard door | $100 to $500 |

A basic full-view door can be fitted in a couple of hours. Doors with closers, lock hardware and adjustments on an imperfect opening take half a day or more.

## Why add a storm door

- **Protects the entry door** from rain, snow and sun, especially wood and painted steel doors.
- **Adds ventilation** with a screen, without letting in bugs.
- **Lets in light** through a full-view glass panel.
- **Reduces drafts** around an older entry door.
- **Adds a layer of security** with a lock and a solid frame.

A caution: on a south- or west-facing door that gets full sun, a storm door with glass can trap heat against a dark entry door and damage the finish or the door. Manufacturers often warn about this. Low-E glass, a lighter door color or a screen-only setup in summer helps.

## Types of storm door

**Full view.** One large glass panel that swaps for a screen in summer. The most popular style.

**Retractable screen.** The screen rolls down from the top of the door when you want air and rolls away when you do not. No swapping panels.

**Mid view and high view.** Part glass, part solid panel. A good choice for families with kids and pets.

**Security storm doors.** Heavy steel frames, strong locks and expanded metal screens. They look different and cost more.

## What makes the install harder

### An out-of-square opening

Storm doors mount to the brickmold or exterior casing around the entry door. If that opening is out of square, the storm door will gap on one side or rub on the other. The Z-bar frame on most storm doors has some adjustment, and beyond that the opening has to be shimmed or corrected.

### Rotted trim

The storm door screws into the exterior trim. If the brickmold is soft, the screws will not hold and the door will sag. That trim has to be replaced first. Our [exterior window trim guide](/blog/exterior-window-trim-replacement-cost/) covers the same kind of trim repair.

### Brick and stone openings

On masonry openings, the storm door frame needs a solid wood or PVC surface to fasten to, not the brick itself. Sometimes new trim is installed first to create a flat, square mounting surface.

### Hardware conflicts

The storm door's handle has to clear the entry door's lockset and knocker. On deep entry doors or doors with large handles, a storm door with an offset or extended frame may be needed.

## How a storm door is installed

1. Measure the opening width at top, middle and bottom, and the height on both sides.
2. Check that the opening is square and the trim is solid. Repair if needed.
3. Choose the hinge side, usually the same as the entry door, and consider which way the wind blows.
4. Fit the drip cap at the top.
5. Install the hinge-side Z-bar, shimmed plumb.
6. Hang the door, then fit the latch-side and top rails.
7. Install the sweep at the bottom and adjust it to the sill.
8. Install the closer or closers and the wind chain, and set the closing speed.
9. Install the handle and lock, and adjust the strike.
10. Check the door closes and latches without slamming, and seals evenly.

## Closers and wind

Storm doors come with a pneumatic closer, and many have two. The closer should be adjusted so the door closes firmly and latches without slamming. A wind chain or a closer with a built-in stop protects the door and the hinges on windy days. Broken closers are one of the most common storm door repairs.

## Choosing a storm door

- **Match the frame color** to the entry door or the trim.
- **Pick the glass**: clear, Low-E or decorative.
- **Pick the hardware**: a keyed lock if security matters.
- **Check the warranty.** Better brands offer longer warranties on frames and closers.
- **Consider the screen**: pet-resistant screens are available on many models.

## When to replace the entry door instead

If your entry door is drafty because it is warped, rotted at the bottom or out of square, a storm door only covers the problem. It may be worth replacing the entry door, or the door and frame, first. ## Maintenance

- Lubricate hinges and the latch once a year.
- Check and adjust the closer each season.
- Clean the sweep and replace it when it wears.
- Swap glass and screen panels on time, if your door uses them, so the glass is not up in a hot summer.
- Check that the screws into the trim are tight.

## Contracts in Pennsylvania

A storm door is home improvement work. In Pennsylvania, contracts over $500 must be in writing, and you have three business days to cancel. No building permit is needed for a storm door on an existing entry.

## Combining with other door work

Because setup is the same, storm door installs are often done with other small exterior jobs: replacing rotted brickmold, adjusting the entry door, a new lockset or a new threshold. Asking for them together saves a trip.

## Measuring for a storm door

Storm doors are sold in standard sizes, most often 32 or 36 inches wide and 80 or 81 inches tall. To choose the right size:

1. Measure the width inside the brickmold or exterior casing at the top, middle and bottom.
2. Measure the height from the sill to the underside of the head trim on both sides.
3. Use the smallest numbers.
4. Check that the trim around the opening is at least about an inch wide and flat, so the frame has something to mount to.

If the opening is between standard sizes, most storm door frames can be adjusted a little. Beyond that, a custom size or new trim is needed. That is why we measure on site instead of ordering from a photo.

## Storm doors on older houses

On older houses, a storm door should not hide the entry door. Full-view glass or a simple wood-look storm door suits most historic entries better than heavy security styles. In historic districts, a storm door on a street-facing entry may need review, especially if it changes the look of a wood door.

## Retractable screen versus swapping panels

Full-view storm doors with interchangeable panels are simple and strong, but someone has to swap the glass and screen twice a year and store the spare panel. Retractable-screen doors keep the screen rolled up in the top of the door and pull it down when you want it, so there is nothing to store. They cost a little more and the screen can be damaged by pets or strong wind if left down. If you open the door for air a lot in spring and fall, many owners find the retractable screen worth it.

## Questions people ask about storm doors

**How long does it take to install a storm door?** A standard door on a good opening usually takes two to four hours. Openings that need trim repair or shimming take longer.

**Can I install a storm door myself?** Many homeowners do. The trickiest parts are a square opening, the hinge rail and adjusting the closer. If the trim is soft or the opening is out of square, a carpenter will save you frustration.

**Do storm doors save energy?** They reduce drafts and protect the main door. The energy savings are modest on a good modern entry door and larger on an old, drafty one.

**Why does my storm door not close all the way?** Usually the closer needs adjustment, the latch is out of line with the strike, or the opening is out of square.
