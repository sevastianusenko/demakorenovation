Most vinyl replacement windows cost somewhere between about $400 and $1,600 per window installed in 2026, and the middle of the market sits around $600 to $1,100. That is a wide range, and the reason is simple: the window itself is only part of the price. The opening it goes into, the glass you choose and the trim work around it decide where your job lands. This guide walks through each of those pieces so a quote makes sense when you get one.

## The short answer, in numbers

Published cost guides for 2026 roughly agree on these ranges for a standard double-hung or slider, installed:

| What you are buying | Typical installed price per window |
|---|---|
| Builder-grade vinyl, insert install, simple opening | $400 to $700 |
| Mid-range vinyl with Low-E glass, insert install | $600 to $1,100 |
| Better vinyl lines, larger sizes, full-frame install | $900 to $1,600 |
| Big picture windows, bays, special shapes | $1,500 and up |

For a whole house, six windows commonly land between about $3,000 and $9,000, and ten to fifteen windows between about $6,000 and $18,000. These figures come from national cost databases such as Fixr and Inch Calculator. Labor in southeastern Pennsylvania tends to sit at or a little above the national middle, so expect local quotes to fall in the upper half of the range rather than the bottom.

We do not publish a price list, because two windows that look the same from the street can be very different jobs. Every estimate we write prices each window and each part of the work on its own line.

## What you are paying for, piece by piece

A replacement window quote has five parts, whether the contractor lists them or not.

1. **The window unit.** Frame, sash, glass and hardware. Vinyl is the lowest-cost frame material, which is why it dominates replacement work.
2. **Removal.** Taking out the old sash or the whole old frame, without damaging the wall around it.
3. **Installation.** Setting the new unit level, plumb and square, shimming it, fastening it and sealing it.
4. **Flashing and insulation.** Tape or membrane that sheds water around the opening, and low-expansion foam or backer rod in the gap.
5. **Trim.** Interior casing and stool, exterior trim or aluminum capping, caulk and cleanup.

When two quotes are far apart, it is almost always because one of them leaves out or skimps on parts 4 and 5.

## Insert or full-frame: the biggest price switch

An **insert** window (also called a pocket or retrofit window) slides into the old frame after the old sashes are removed. The old frame, interior trim and exterior trim stay. It is faster and cheaper, and it works well when the old frame is solid and square.

A **full-frame** replacement takes the window out down to the rough opening. That costs more, usually a few hundred dollars more per window, because there is more labor and new trim on both sides. It is the right choice when the old frame is rotted, when you want back the glass area an insert gives up, or when water has been getting into the wall.

We wrote a separate guide on [insert versus full-frame replacement](/blog/insert-vs-full-frame-window-replacement/) if you want the details. The quick rule: push a screwdriver into the bottom of the old frame and the sill outside. If it sinks in, an insert is just covering a problem.

## What makes a vinyl window cost more

### Size and style

Standard double-hungs and sliders are the cheapest. Casements cost more because of the crank hardware. Picture windows cost more because of the glass area. Bay and bow windows are small construction projects in their own right, with a roof, a seat board and support.

### Glass package

Double-pane glass with a Low-E coating and argon gas is the normal choice in Pennsylvania. Triple-pane adds cost and weight. It makes sense on the north side of a house or next to a busy road, less so everywhere else. Tempered glass is required by code near doors, stairs, tubs and in some large windows, and adds to the price of those units.

### The opening and the wall

This is where old houses cost more. Brick and stone openings are rarely square and are often a different size top to bottom. Stucco needs care so it does not crack when the old window comes out. Plaster walls inside need the casing removed and reset without breaking the plaster. On the brick house we did in the Douglassville area of Berks County, each opening was measured separately and the new units sit on new stone sills.

### Exterior trim

Many older windows have wood trim outside that has rotted or been painted a dozen times. You can wrap it in custom-bent aluminum, replace it in PVC, or rebuild it in wood. Each is a separate line on a good quote. If the trim is soft, wrapping it only hides the rot for a few years.

### Lead paint

If your house was built before 1978, federal EPA rules require a lead-safe certified firm for window replacement, because taking out old windows disturbs painted surfaces. That means containment plastic, careful cleanup and a final check. It adds time and cost, and it protects your family. Ask any contractor who quotes a pre-1978 house whether they are certified.

### Second-story and hard access

Windows over a porch roof, above a steep slope or in a tall stair hall need ladders, planks or staging. Expect those to cost more than a first-floor bedroom window.

## Why one cheap quote can cost more later

The cheapest vinyl window jobs usually save money in three places: no flashing, too much expanding foam and caulk instead of trim. All three show up later. Water gets behind the frame, foam bows the jambs so the sash sticks, and caulk joints open up after a couple of winters.

A good install looks like this, in order:

1. Remove the old unit and clean out the opening.
2. Check the sill and framing for rot and repair it.
3. Install a sloped sill pan or flashing at the bottom so any water that gets in drains out.
4. Set the window on shims, check it for level, plumb and square, and fasten it.
5. Fill the gap with low-expansion foam or backer rod, not a can of the strong stuff.
6. Flash the sides and top in the right order, so each layer laps over the one below like shingles.
7. Trim inside and out, then caulk only the joints that need it.

We follow that sequence on every window, whether it is one window in a bathroom or a row of them in a new addition.

## Vinyl versus other materials

Vinyl is not the only option, and it is worth knowing where it fits.

- **Vinyl** is the lowest cost, needs no painting and performs well for most houses. Colors are limited and dark colors can be more expensive.
- **Fiberglass** costs more, is stiffer and handles heat and dark colors better.
- **Wood and wood-clad** windows, like Marvin or Andersen wood lines, cost the most and suit older and historic houses where the look matters.
- **Composite** frames, such as Andersen's Fibrex, sit between vinyl and wood.

If you are weighing brands, our [Marvin versus Andersen guide](/blog/marvin-vs-andersen-replacement-windows/) covers the two we install most.

## How to read a window quote

When you have a quote in front of you, check for these lines:

- The brand, product line and glass package for each window.
- Insert or full-frame, window by window.
- What happens to the exterior trim: new PVC, aluminum capping or reused.
- Interior trim: reused, new casing, or new stool and apron.
- Flashing method, written out.
- Lead-safe practices, for pre-1978 houses.
- Disposal of old windows.
- An allowance or unit price for rot repair, in case the sill or framing is bad once the old window is out.

If a quote is a single number for "replace 12 windows," ask for the breakdown. A contractor who knows what they are doing can give it to you.

In Pennsylvania, home improvement contracts over $500 must be in writing, you have three business days to cancel, and for jobs over $1,000 the deposit cannot be more than one third of the contract price plus the cost of special-order materials. Those rules come from the Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act and are worth knowing before you sign anything.

## Replacing a few windows or all of them

Doing all the windows at once usually costs less per window, because setup, travel and trim work are spread over more units. But replacing the worst windows first is a perfectly good plan. Most houses have one or two sides that take the weather and wear out sooner. If you go in stages, keep the same brand and product line so the windows match.

## Does it pay back?

New windows rarely pay for themselves in energy savings alone in a few years. The real benefits are comfort, less drafting, less condensation, windows that open and lock, and a house that sells more easily. If your old windows still work and the frames are sound, new storm windows or a good tune-up may be a better use of money. If the frames are rotting or water is getting into the wall, replacement is the fix.

## Questions people ask about vinyl window cost

**How long does vinyl window replacement take?** An experienced crew usually replaces several insert windows in a day. Full-frame windows with new trim inside and out take longer, often two to four per day depending on the wall.

**Do I need a permit to replace windows in Pennsylvania?** Usually not when the new window goes into the same opening. Making an opening bigger, cutting a header, or changing a bedroom egress window is different, so check with your township or ask us at the estimate.

**How long do vinyl windows last?** Good vinyl windows commonly last 20 to 30 years. Seals between the glass panes are usually the first thing to fail, which shows as fogging between the panes.

**Can you replace just one window?** Yes. One window costs more per unit than ten, because the visit and setup are the same, but it is a normal job for us.

**Is the estimate free?** Yes. We come out, measure every opening, check the frames and sills, and send a written estimate with each window on its own line.
